What I don't do — and why I'll tell you on the first call

I don't determine who is responsible for an incident, and I don't prepare findings for a court, insurance, or other proceeding. In Texas that is licensed investigative work under Chapter 1702 of the Occupations Code, and I don't hold that license.

Response and hardening are a different thing, and the same statute says so directly: reviewing computer-based data to prepare for or respond to a cybersecurity event is expressly outside the licensing requirement (Tex. Occ. Code §1702.104(c)).

If what you need crosses that line, I'll say so on the first call and refer you to someone licensed. Everything I've already captured goes with you.

Straight answers

The questions everybody asks.

"Can you tell us who did this?"

No — and in Texas I'm not permitted to. Determining who's responsible, or preparing findings for a court or an insurance proceeding, requires a private investigator license under Chapter 1702. I do response and hardening, which the same statute expressly exempts. If you need that work, I'll say so on the first call and refer you to someone licensed.

"We're too small to be a target."

Small shops aren't targeted — they're swept up. Automated scanning doesn't check your headcount before it tries your remote-access port.

"We already have an IT guy."

Good. Keep him. This isn't help desk work — it's the layer above it, and most break-fix vendors will tell you the same thing if you ask them directly.

"Isn't this going to be a huge project?"

"Can you guarantee we won't get hit?"
