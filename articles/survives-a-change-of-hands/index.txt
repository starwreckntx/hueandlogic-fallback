22:T371b,<p>This week, Outlook went down for nearly a day, and millions of inboxes, calendars and Teams meetings went with it. The next afternoon, every departing flight in the United States was grounded for more than four hours because a telecommunications link failed. A host returns HTTP 402 and a website goes dark. A frequency gets jammed and a beacon goes silent. A model gets deprecated and a workflow stops mid-task. Nobody was hacked in any of those cases. Whatever carried the work was never the operator's to begin with.</p>
<p>You don't own radio waves. Spectrum is licensed, shared, and contested, and anyone with a stronger transmitter can take a channel. The same is true of every host, cloud, inbox, and model API. They are all media, and all of them can be contested, repriced, or switched off.</p>
<p>What you can own is the method: how work is written, sealed, logged, and checked. A method that doesn't depend on the medium survives any change of hands. This article lays one out concretely: sealed handoff packets, hash-chained logs, cross-model review, and a list of what it still doesn't prove.</p>

<h2>You don't own the medium. You own the method.</h2>
<p>Every channel your work travels on belongs to someone else, and each can be contested a different way. The method is what stays the same across all of them.</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Medium</th><th>How it gets contested</th><th>What the method keeps</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Leased telecom lines</td><td>On Nov 13, 2026, a telecommunications outage hit air route traffic control centers, and the FAA stopped every departure nationwide (<a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/faa-ground-stop-lifted-why-were-us-flights-grounded-and-whats-next">Al Jazeera</a>).</td><td>A record that doesn't depend on any one line staying up.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Email (Outlook)</td><td>On Nov 12–13, 2026, Outlook, Teams, SharePoint and Copilot were down for nearly 24 hours. Microsoft blamed a traffic surge and a server-health component (<a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2026/11/12/microsoft-outage-outlook-aws-google.html">CNBC</a>).</td><td>The packet is a file. Its hash checks the same whether it arrives by email, USB or radio.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Radio spectrum</td><td>Licensed, shared and jammable. A stronger transmitter takes the channel.</td><td>A signed frame verifies on any frequency it gets through on.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Web host</td><td>Payment lapses, the account is suspended, and the host returns HTTP 402.</td><td>Static files plus a deploy step that targets any host.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Model API</td><td>Deprecated, repriced, rate-limited or quietly changed.</td><td>The packet states what was verified, what was inferred and what is open, so any model can pick it up.</td></tr>
<tr><td>Cloud storage</td><td>Quota hit, terms changed, account locked.</td><td>Content-addressed files that copy to any disk without losing their names.</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>The air-traffic case shows the problem clearly. At 3:54 p.m. ET on Friday, Nov 13, the FAA stopped every departure in the country after a telecommunications outage hit the air route traffic control centers (<a href="https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/11/14/faa-ground-stop-lifted-why-were-us-flights-grounded-and-whats-next">Al Jazeera</a>). It was the longest flight stoppage in 25 years. It ran more than four hours and ended around 8:15 p.m. ET (<a href="https://www.washingtonpost.com/business/2026/11/14/us-flights-grounded-back-to-normal/3b2114da-e022-11f1-912c-4fbb80d73f7f_story.html">Washington Post</a>). Of 50,000 flights scheduled that day, about 12,000 could not be completed (<a href="https://www.cbsnews.com/news/faa-ground-stop-canceled-flights-travelers-what-we-know/">CBS News</a>). The Transportation Secretary said upgrade work on an old telecommunication system, an archaic copper wire switch, could be the cause (<a href="https://www.cnn.com/2026/11/14/politics/faa-ground-stop-what-we-know">CNN</a>). As of this writing, that is a possible cause, not a confirmed one.</p>
<p>It wasn't the first time this fall. On Sept 21, the primary circuit into Philadelphia TRACON failed, and the backup turned out to have been cut too. Both ran along the same Amtrak right-of-way, where a construction crew severed the fiber (<a href="https://aeroxplorer.com/articles/faa-equipment-outage-grounds-flights-at-newark-jfk-and-philadelphia-airports.php">AeroXplorer</a>).</p>
<p>In both cases the radar worked and the controllers were at their posts. The aircraft were fine. The medium between them failed, and the operator didn't own that medium: a leased line, a shared trench, a switch someone else was upgrading. Redundancy you don't control is one medium counted twice.</p>

<p>Four rules make the method independent of any medium:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The selector is not the generator.</strong> The model that produces work never decides whether that work is accepted.</li>
<li><strong>The narrator explains, never authors.</strong> A model can explain a decision after it's made. It doesn't make the decision.</li>
<li><strong>AI explains; it never authorizes.</strong> Approvals are operator ruling lines in the ledger. Dispatch, spend and promotion each need their own line.</li>
<li><strong>Default deny.</strong> Nothing executes across a trust boundary without a human saying yes.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The method, in eight steps</h2>
<p>None of these steps names a vendor, a host or a frequency. That's deliberate. Each one works the same on whatever medium happens to be available.</p>
<ol>
<li><p><strong>One intake format, many doors.</strong> Every model and node submits to the same intake, and the intake accepts packets through more than one door: an API write, an email to a labeled inbox, a local outbox folder for machines with no cloud access. If one door closes, the format doesn't change. Packets just come in through another door.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Write every handoff as a sealed packet.</strong> One file per handoff, the same fields every time:</p>
<pre><code>PACKET/1
from: &lt;model or node&gt;   to: &lt;model or node&gt;   scope: &lt;work&gt;
verified: claims traceable to something read this session
inferred: claims derived, not read
open: unresolved items carried forward</code></pre>
<p>The split between <code>verified</code> and <code>inferred</code> is the whole point. A model that can't say which of its claims it actually read can't be handed work.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Seal it with its own hash.</strong> Hash the body, then append the hash as the last line. Anyone can re-check it by hashing everything except that line:</p>
<pre><code>printf 'sha256: %s\n' "$(sha256sum &lt; packet.md | cut -d' ' -f1)" &gt;&gt; packet.md
head -n -1 packet.md | sha256sum      # must match the last line</code></pre>
<p>On macOS, use <code>sed '$d' packet.md | shasum -a 256</code>, because BSD <code>head</code> has no negative count. If the result starts with <code>e3b0c442</code>, the input was empty. Check before publishing.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Name files by content, not by counter.</strong> Name each packet <code>&lt;date&gt;_&lt;slug&gt;_&lt;first 8 hex of its hash&gt;.md</code>. Counters collide when two nodes write at the same moment. Hashes don't collide, and they keep the same name on any disk.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Keep one append-only, hash-chained ledger.</strong> Each line carries the hash of the line before it, so a deleted or edited line breaks the chain where it happened. One process writes the ledger: the runner. Models propose and the runner records. A rule change is a new rulebook version plus a new ruling line, never an edit to the old one.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Hash and log on receipt.</strong> Anything that enters the system gets hashed and logged before it gets used. That covers a packet, a render, a media file, or a client document. If it isn't in the ledger, it didn't arrive.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Review across model families.</strong> Three roles. A coordinator writes the job packet, an executor does the work, and a blind verifier writes the verdict without seeing the executor's reasoning. The verifier must come from a different <em>root model family</em>: whoever made the weights, not whoever resells them. A malformed verdict is logged as <code>VERIFIER_ERROR</code>, not as a fail. A new model earns promotion on volume: 5 shadow jobs before it executes, 10 adjudicated verifications before it verifies. High-risk public work needs two passes.</p></li>
<li><p><strong>Rehearse the move.</strong> Every medium will fail eventually, so test the switch before you need it. Keep a plain static copy of anything public, a deploy step that targets more than one host, and a reachability check that runs from any terminal:</p>
<pre><code>ssh -o BatchMode=yes -o ConnectTimeout=5 &lt;host&gt; true &amp;&amp; echo REACHABLE</code></pre>
<p>Then move something on purpose. If the method only works on the medium it was built on, it's a dependency.</p></li>
</ol>

<h2>Worked example: one packet, three media</h2>
<p>The same 2 KB sealed packet has to reach a verifier while the usual channels fail one after another. The medium changes three times. The hash doesn't change at all.</p>
<table><thead><tr><th>Medium</th><th>What contests it</th><th>What arrives</th><th>Check on receipt</th></tr></thead><tbody>
<tr><td>Email</td><td>The provider is down, like Outlook in the cases above</td><td>Nothing, so the sender switches doors</td><td>None needed</td></tr>
<tr><td>Web host</td><td>The account lapses and the host returns HTTP 402</td><td>Nothing, so DNS gets pointed at a static fallback and the packet goes out on another host</td><td><code>sha256</code> matches the seal line</td></tr>
<tr><td>Packet radio, 1200-baud AFSK</td><td>The channel is shared and can be jammed</td><td>The same bytes, slowly: 16,384 bits takes at least 14 seconds before framing overhead</td><td><code>sha256</code> matches the seal line</td></tr>
</tbody></table>
<p>The verifier never needs to know which medium delivered the packet. It hashes the body, compares the result to the last line, and logs the receipt. A failed check means the packet is rejected, whatever channel carried it. The medium decides how fast the bytes arrive. The method decides whether they can be trusted.</p>

<h2>What this still doesn't prove</h2>
<p>A method that only lists its strengths isn't on the record, so here are the limits.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Integrity isn't delivery.</strong> A jammed channel is still jammed, and a cut fiber is still cut. The method guarantees that what arrives can be checked. It can't guarantee that anything arrives. Delivery depends on having more than one medium that doesn't share a failure path.</li>
<li><strong>A hash proves a record didn't change. It doesn't prove who wrote it.</strong> The chain catches tampering after the fact. Signing authorship is a separate layer.</li>
<li><strong>Agreement isn't independence.</strong> Review across model families catches a lot. But two models trained on overlapping data can agree for the same wrong reason. A shadow auditor that watches for herding helps, and it still can't prove a consensus is independent.</li>
<li><strong>The chain has a start date.</strong> Work done before the ledger existed has no provenance, and nothing in the method recovers it.</li>
<li><strong>Coverage depends on the packet getting written.</strong> A session that never submits a packet leaves no record. Emitting packets automatically from the ledger, with a harvester checking for gaps, closes most of that hole. It doesn't close all of it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Could they?</h2>
<p>If your models, your host, your inbox and your billing account all changed hands tomorrow, could the next operator pick up your work from the records alone? The medium will change. The question is whether your method survives the change. Could they?</p>
<p>Read it. Test it. Then argue with me.</p>
